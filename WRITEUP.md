# TB Detection from Chest X-Rays: Does Pretraining Reduce the Generalization Gap?

In small-data medical imaging, ImageNet pretraining is usually judged on raw accuracy. I wanted to ask a narrower question: does it also narrow the gap between training and test performance, which is the actual signature of overfitting. Using the Montgomery County and Shenzhen chest X-ray datasets (800 images combined), I trained five models on an identical 90/10 split: a baseline CNN from scratch, ResNet-18 from scratch, ResNet-18 fine-tuned from ImageNet weights, and the same scratch/pretrained pair for DenseNet-121. The two scratch models matter because they separate the effect of pretraining from the effect of simply using a bigger architecture. Without them, any gain from the pretrained models could just as easily be capacity, not pretraining.

The baseline CNN overfit as expected: 77.6% train accuracy against 68.75% test, an 8.9-point gap. The first thing that didn't fit was architecture alone. ResNet-18 trained from scratch with a uniform learning rate actually had a *larger* gap than the baseline, not a smaller one. More capacity with no other regularization just made the overfitting worse.

Learning rate turned out to matter more than either of those. Pretrained ResNet-18 under a single uniform rate fell apart: 96% train, 67.5% test, a 28.6-point gap. The rate was tuned for random weights and was too aggressive for weights that already encoded something useful, so it wiped out the pretrained structure in the first few epochs. Switching to differential learning rates (a low rate for the backbone, a higher rate for the classification head) fixed that. But when I ran the same differential-rate setup on the scratch ResNet-18, which has no pretrained structure to protect at all, it also improved by a lot, and the gap dropped close to zero. So differential learning rates weren't just protecting pretrained features, they were also acting as a general regularizer, independent of what the weights started as.

That's why holding learning rate strategy fixed is the only comparison in this project that's actually controlled for one variable. Under that comparison, pretraining gave a real but modest accuracy gain on both architectures (+2.5 points for ResNet-18, +8.75 for DenseNet-121), while making the gap worse in both cases (+10.6 points and +6.5 points). That's the opposite of what I expected going in, where pretraining should help generalization, not hurt it. The direction of the effect held across both architectures, but the size didn't: DenseNet-121 got a bigger accuracy gain for a smaller gap cost than ResNet-18 did.

I also computed AUC, sensitivity, and specificity for all five conditions. Every model was better at correctly ruling out TB than at catching it, which is the wrong way round for a screening tool, since a missed case is worse than a false alarm. That points at the 0.5 decision threshold used throughout: it's probably not the right operating point here, and a lower threshold that trades some false positives for fewer missed cases would likely make more sense clinically.

The main limitations: an 80-image test set with real run-to-run noise, data that behaves like it comes from a small number of sources with no external validation, one fixed split rather than cross-validation, and hyperparameters chosen by informal comparison rather than a proper search.
